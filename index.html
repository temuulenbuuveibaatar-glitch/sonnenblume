01 · Who we are

A sunflower tracks the sun and shields its seeds at the core.

We are Team Sonnenblume, a NASA Space Apps team building the self-health system we think long missions need. Sonnenblume is German for sunflower: a flower that keeps turning toward the sun while protecting what matters most at its centre.

Our software works the same way. It keeps watching the hostile environment around the crew, from radiation and isolation to a closed habitat and distance from Earth. It also protects the real core of a mission: the physical and mental health of the people on board.

On the station, a flight surgeon is a call away. On Mars, a message takes 3 to 22 minutes each way. So we designed for the moment when the crew is its own first responder.

02 · Who it's for

Built for the people who can't wait for Earth.

Astronauts

Crew members on long-duration missions who carry most of the job of spotting changes in their own health. Takes about two minutes a day.

Crew medical officers

The crewmate trained to respond first. They get automatic alerts when two or more indicators reach Act now, plus the full picture to act on.

Flight surgeons on Earth

The ground team receives the same data after the comms delay, plus a clean export of every reading for review.

Analog & training crews

Antarctic stations, analog habitats and classrooms can use the same routine to practise self-assessment before anyone flies.

03 · How it works

Measure. Understand. Act. Escalate when it matters.

  1. 1

    Measure

    Each day the astronaut logs 12 indicators: heart rate, blood pressure, blood oxygen, temperature, fasting blood sugar, sleep, mood, exercise, fluid intake, radiation dose, a vision check and weekly body mass. Each field shows its normal range as you type.

  2. 2

    Understand

    Every reading is colour-coded Nominal, Watch or Act now. Three extra checks catch what a single number misses: sudden changes from your personal baseline, a rebound from a dangerous value within 24 h, and critical readings that may be a faulty sensor or an emergency.

  3. 3

    See it on the body

    An anatomical body map lights up the affected system: brain, eyes, heart and vessels, lungs, digestion and kidneys, muscles, bones, immune system, and a radiation aura around the body. Tap any organ to learn what it does, how space affects it and what to do now.

  4. 4

    Act

    "What to do now" lists clear, plain-language steps for each flagged indicator. The guidance changes with the mission: near Earth it says call the flight surgeon; on Mars it says start the onboard protocol first.

  5. 5

    Escalate

    When two or more indicators hit Act now, Sonnenblume automatically alerts the crew leader and medical officer. The flight surgeon on Earth gets a copy after the comms delay, and a follow-up goes out when values recover.

04 · The science

Five hazards of spaceflight, one routine that watches them all.

NASA's Human Research Program groups the risks of spaceflight into five hazards. Each one maps to something the crew can measure every day.

Space radiation

Galactic cosmic rays and solar storms. Tracked as a daily dosimeter reading against a 600 mSv career budget, with a solar-storm warning when the dose spikes.

Isolation & confinement

Small crews, monotony, distance from family. Tracked through sleep and mood, with an extra flag when several bad nights or low days come in a row.

Distance from Earth

No quick return and slow comms. Every environment carries its real one-way delay, and the advice and alerts change to match it.

Gravity fields

Fluid shifts, bone and muscle loss, eye changes (SANS). Tracked with blood pressure, exercise, body mass and a daily vision check.

Hostile closed environment

Cabin air, CO₂, dust and germs. Tracked through blood oxygen, temperature and symptoms, linked to cabin checks and isolation advice.

Thresholds are general adult reference ranges for demonstration. Radiation rates are approximate published figures for the ISS, Mars cruise and surface (MSL-RAD) and the lunar surface (Chang'e-4). Sonnenblume is a prototype, not a medical device.

05 · Built for deep space

What deep-space medicine still lacks, and what Sonnenblume does about it.

NASA calls the goal Earth-Independent Medical Operations: crews beyond the Moon must handle medical care with no real-time help, no resupply and no evacuation. Today's station medicine was built for the opposite.

No real-time doctor

Mars is 3 to 22 minutes away by radio, and a solar conjunction cuts the link for about two weeks every 26 months. Sonnenblume: an Earth-independent mode, live delay and blackout simulation, and an outbox that shows when each message reaches Earth and the earliest possible reply.

No resupply

A 2024 study found 54 of the 91 ISS medicines have a shelf life of 3 years or less, so most would expire before a Mars mission ends. Sonnenblume: an onboard pharmacy that tracks stock and flags every medicine that expires before the mission does.

No evacuation

The crew medical officer must act first, alone. Sonnenblume: 67 guided protocols and 119 medicines. Each protocol timestamps every step, sends a log to the crew and Earth, and links to the reading that triggered it.

No internet, little bandwidth

Sonnenblume: works fully offline once loaded, with no outside fonts or scripts, and status reports about 1 KB in size for weak deep-space links.

Private by design

Health data never leaves the device unless the crew member exports it. Read the Privacy Charter.

Sources: NASA Human Research Program, Earth-Independent Medical Operations ConOps (NTRS 20240001402); Diaz et al., npj Microgravity 2024, doi 10.1038/s41526-024-00414-3; NASA/JPL Mars solar conjunction notices.

06 · Guide

Your first mission day in seven steps.

  1. Choose your mission. At the top of the console, pick where you are: space station, deep-space transit, lunar base, lunar rover or Mars base. This sets your normal radiation rate and how long Earth takes to reply.
  2. Do your morning check. Open Log measurements. After waking, before coffee or exercise, record sleep, resting heart rate, blood pressure, blood oxygen, temperature and fasting blood sugar. Each field shows its normal range as you type.
  3. Read your body map. Back on the Dashboard, green means nominal, amber means watch and red means act now. Tap any organ for its status and advice. Switch layers (organs, skeleton, muscles, vessels) to explore.
  4. Follow "What to do now". Every flagged indicator comes with plain steps. A banner at the top tells you whether to start the onboard protocol or contact the flight surgeon.
  5. Log the rest of your day. Add exercise, fluids, mood, your vision check and the dosimeter reading in the evening. Completing your routine earns XP, streaks and badges.
  6. Watch your trends. In Trends, switch between Day, Week and Month. Day shows every reading you saved today, so sudden swings stand out.
  7. Trust the alert. If two or more indicators hit Act now, your crew leader and medical officer are alerted automatically. You can also press Alert now at any time. Use Export data to send the full record.

Good to know

Where is my data stored?

Only in this browser on this device. Nothing is uploaded. Use Export data to share it.

Why does it say Watch when my values look normal?

A sudden swing is a warning sign too. If a reading was at Act now within the last 24 h, or jumped far from your usual, it stays on Watch until a steady re-check.

What does "Critical, check sensor" mean?

The value is outside a survivable or plausible range. Re-seat the sensor and measure again. If it's confirmed, treat it as an emergency.

Is this a medical device?

No. It's a prototype that shows how self-assessment could work in space. Thresholds are general reference ranges, not personal medical limits.

Can I try it without real measurements?

Yes. The console opens with 31 days of demo data. Try entering extreme values, then normal ones, to see the alerts and sudden-change detection. Reset demo data restores it.
